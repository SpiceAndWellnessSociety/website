## Introduction

The sizzling of tadka [*frying spices, herbs, and garlic in oil to add flavor to a dish*], the rich, creamy swirl of paneer butter masala, the warmth of fresh roti off the tawa [*stove*]—South Asian food is more than nourishment. It’s comfort. It’s tradition. It’s home.

But while these flavors are nostalgic staples in many Indian households, they also come with an uncomfortable truth: South Asians around the world are facing rising rates of heart disease, diabetes, and obesity. Diabetes and insulin resistance affects up to 20% of the South Asian population[^stanford]. Among young South Asians, one in four heart attacks occurs before the age of 40, and half happen before the age of 50[^stanford]. The very dishes that once nourished generations are now quietly contributing to a growing health crisis.

So what changed?

Traditional South Asian diets were once rooted in seasonal ingredients, modest portions, and physically active lifestyles. But today, many of us live far from the farms and spice markets our grandparents knew. Our lives are more sedentary, our meals more processed, and our plates overloaded with refined carbs and saturated fats; especially for those of us living abroad.

This article explores how cultural staples like rice, roti, and rich curries—when consumed out of context—can impact modern health. By finding ways to honor tradition while embracing evolving lifestyles and bodies, we may be able to redefine the path to better health—without letting go of our roots.

## The Evolution of the South Asian Diet

For generations, South Asian diets were deeply connected to the land and the rhythms of everyday life. Meals were crafted around what was available seasonally—fresh vegetables, lentils, whole grains, and spices not just for flavor, but for their medicinal properties. In fact, commonly used grains are consumed in different ways across various regions of the country, tailored to suit seasonal availability and local conditions[^holmboe]. *Ghee* (clarified butter) was used sparingly, and sweets were reserved for special occasions. Physical movement was woven into the fabric of daily livingr through farming, household labor, or walking to markets and temples.

However, that context has shifted dramatically. Today, many South Asians live in urban centers far from the agricultural roots of their ancestors. Immigration, modernization, and busy work lives have brought convenience to the forefront. Traditional meals—once balanced and portioned—are now often heavy with refined carbs, oils, and processed ingredients. In a recent study, research indicated that South Asians who had moved abroad had significantly altered their traditional meal patterns after relocating, reducing their daily intake of hot meals from three to approximately one and a half. Additionally, the youngest age group (under 40 years) tended to use more fats, including oil, butter, and margarine, in their meals[^holmboe]. What was once an occasional indulgence has, in many households, become an everyday staple.

This evolution isn't inherently negative—change is natural, but the issue arises when traditional recipes, now consumed in a completely different lifestyle context, contribute to rising health risks. Many of the ingredients and cooking methods that once sustained physically active lives no longer align with the realities of sedentary work environments and fast-paced schedules.

## Cultural Foods in a Modern Context

Many traditional dishes were created to evoke comfort and warmth, while being rich in flavor and rooted in history. However, from a nutritional standpoint, some of these beloved staples are less than ideal when consumed in excess or without balance. In India, commonly used grains are wheat, rice, sorghum, and pearl millet, all of which are extremely carbohydrate-rich[^holmboe]. White rice, in particular, is a refined carbohydrate that rapidly raises blood sugar levels and offers little fiber compared to whole grains. The same study found that average grain and pulse consumption in India ranges from just over 300 to nearly 600 grams per day per Consumption Unit (CU), indicating that in many regions, these staples—especially rice—are consumed in large quantities. Such high intake, particularly of refined grains, is associated with elevated blood sugar and a greater risk of metabolic disorders, highlighting how unbalanced traditional eating patterns can contribute to long-term health concerns[^holmboe].

Similarly, curries, while flavorful, are often made with generous amounts of cream, butter, or coconut milk, contributing significantly to saturated fat intake. Research conducted in Birmingham found that South Asians were more likely than Europeans and Afro-Caribbeans to cook with methods like deep frying and to use ingredients such as butter, ghee, and full-fat milk, all of which contribute to higher saturated fat intake[^holmboe]. While these components are not inherently harmful, the frequent and unmoderated consumption of such ingredients can tip the scale toward poor metabolic health over time.

In today’s South Asian food, traditional recipes are often amplified—larger portions, richer ingredients, and more frequent indulgence. Celebratory dishes once reserved for special occasions are now everyday meals, thanks in part to convenience and changing lifestyles. This shift blurs the line between occasional indulgences and overindulgence. For example, eating a heavily spiced, creamy curry with white rice multiple times a week can strain the body's ability to maintain healthy blood sugar and cholesterol levels. This excess consumption has led to rising rates of diet-related conditions such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and heart disease—especially in communities that continue to hold these dishes at the center of daily life without adapting portion sizes or ingredients[^bhupathiraju]. A recent WHO report projected that although South Asians would make up just 25% of the global population by 2020, they would account for more than half of all cardiovascular-related deaths worldwide[^stanford].

## Conclusion

South Asian food is rich in tradition, flavor, and comfort—but in today’s context, it requires a more mindful approach. As our lifestyles have shifted toward less physical activity and greater access to processed foods, traditional meals—once balanced and occasional—have become everyday indulgences, contributing to a rise in health issues.

However, not all aspects of the South Asian diet need changing. In fact, new research highlights the benefits of a healthy plant-based diet, showing a strong inverse relationship between such eating patterns and the accumulation of ectopic fat—visceral, liver, and pericardial fat—which is a major risk factor for cardiometabolic disease[^bhupathiraju]. This is especially relevant for South Asians, who tend to have higher levels of visceral and pericardial fat compared to other ethnic groups. The findings suggest that embracing the naturally vegetarian roots of many South Asian diets—rich in legumes, vegetables, spices, and whole grains—can play a role in preventing chronic disease, particularly when those diets emphasize quality over quantity.

Rather than completely eliminating cherished foods like ghee, white rice, or curries, the South Asian diet calls for moderation and adaptation. This means rebalancing plates with more fiber-rich vegetables and whole grains, reducing portion sizes, and being mindful of how often and how much we indulge in richer dishes. It also means emphasizing the parts of our culinary heritage that promote health—like quality plant-based meals, spice diversity, and traditional cooking methods that don't rely on excess oil or fat.

Tradition and wellness don’t have to be at odds; with balance, they can continue to sustain us.
