Cultural beliefs have long been a cornerstone of health practices in South Asian communities, influencing how individuals perceive illness, seek treatment, and maintain well-being. This cultural lens blends ancient traditions, like Ayurveda and holistic remedies, with modern healthcare practices, creating a unique perspective in the South Asian community’s medical approaches.

The importance of these beliefs is best exemplified by Vijay Wadhawan, Senior Vice President of Health and Wellness at Environics Research, in his article, *“Harmonizing Health: Cultural Reflections on South Asian Heritage Month and Healthcare in Canada.”* Wadhawan explores how South Asian cultural values continue to define healthcare approaches in both traditional settings and immigrant communities. Utilizing Wadhawan's insights, this article will explore three key themes: the enduring influence of traditional medicine, nutrition and its role in health, and the impact of family and societal norms on health decisions.

## Traditional Medicine and its Enduring Influence

Traditional medicine systems such as Ayurveda and Unani have played a foundational role in South Asian health practices for centuries. These systems emphasize holistic care, natural remedies, and balancing bodily energies like "doshas" to maintain well-being. These practices are still relevant in immigrant communities today. For example, South Asian families often use herbal remedies like turmeric milk, ginger tea, and neem to address common ailments such as colds, fevers, or inflammation[^kumar].

In his article, Wadhawan shares the story of a South Asian mother in Canada who routinely gave her children traditional herbal teas for seasonal illnesses, alongside over-the-counter medications if needed. This illustrates the integration of traditional approaches with modern medicine, creating a culturally holistic approach[^wadhawan].

While these practices are time-honored and align with cultural values, they can sometimes conflict with modern medicine, specifically when patients prioritize traditional methods over modern treatments. This highlights the increasing need for healthcare providers to acknowledge these practices and educate patients on well-rounded integration.

## Nutrition and Health Practices

In South Asian cultures, food is seen as more than just a source of energy—it’s considered a way to heal and keep the body balanced. Many of these dietary practices are based on Ayurvedic principles, which focus on how different foods affect the body. However, some traditional diets, which are often high in carbohydrates or saturated fats, can increase the risk of health issues like diabetes and heart disease[^molina].

Wadhawan shared an example of a South Asian family adjusting their diet to better manage diabetes. They still enjoyed cultural dishes like dal and parathas, but made them healthier by using whole grains and cutting back on ghee (clarified butter). This shows how families are finding ways to combine traditional food with modern health advice[^wadhawan].

Healthcare providers can help by respecting cultural food traditions and offering practical suggestions, such as swapping ingredients or changing portion sizes, to reduce health risks while keeping meals culturally meaningful.

## Family and Societal Norms in Health Decisions

Family and community also play a huge role in healthcare decisions for many South Asians. Health is often seen as a shared responsibility, with family members working together to make decisions and provide care. This focus on family reflects cultural values of interdependence and collective support[^kumar].

Wadhawan highlights a story about an elderly South Asian woman in Canada who refused to move into a nursing home because, in her culture, elders are traditionally cared for within the family. Her children rearranged their work schedules to care for her at home, showing how deeply these values influence decisions, even in immigrant families[^wadhawan].

This focus on family can create challenges in Western healthcare systems, where decisions often emphasize individual choice. For instance, some families may resist options like long-term care facilities or hospice services because they conflict with cultural expectations. Healthcare providers can work through these challenges by having open conversations and finding solutions that honor the family’s values while providing the necessary care.

## Conclusion

Cultural beliefs remain significant in shaping health practices within South Asian communities, blending traditional approaches with modern medical systems. The enduring influence of practices like Ayurveda, culturally significant dietary habits, and the central role of family in health decisions exemplify the unique ways South Asians navigate their healthcare journeys.

By exploring these themes through Vijay Wadhawan's examples, it seems important to acknowledge and respect such cultural values in healthcare.
