Recent studies estimate that nearly seven in ten adults in South Asia are deficient in vitamin D, despite living in regions with abundant sunlight. A 2021 meta-analysis found prevalence rates as high as 73% in the region, making vitamin D deficiency one of the most widespread nutritional problems in the region[^siddiqee]. This article explores the growing epidemic this is and it’s prevalence, underlying causes, and health implications of vitamin D deficiency in South Asian populations, drawing on recent research.

The systematic review published in BCM Public Health estimated that roughly 68 percent of adults in South Asia suffer from vitamin D deficiency, defined by serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels below 20 ng/mL. Country-specific data reveal similarly concerning figures, with prevalence reaching approximately 74 percent in Pakistan, 67 percent in both India and Bangladesh, 57 percent in Nepal, and 48 percent in Sri Lanka[^siddiqee]. These findings suggest that vitamin D deficiency is not confined to a single nation but represents an overall regional public-health challenge. Earlier research, such as a study in The Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism, confirms these findings; in a cohort of urban Asian Indians, 94 percent had serum vitamin D levels below 20 ng/mL, with an average of just 9.8 ng/mL[^shah]. Such consistently low concentrations highlight the severity of the deficiency across diverse populations and settings.

The prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in South Asia is likely multi-faceted. Limited sun exposure, caused by urbanization, indoor lifestyles, and cultural clothing practices, restricts the skin’s ability to synthesize vitamin D[^siddiqee]. Air pollution and seasonal weather patterns, including monsoons, further reduce ultraviolet B radiation reaching the skin. Furthermore, darker skin pigmentation, common among South Asian populations, naturally reduces vitamin D synthesis, requiring longer exposure to sunlight ot produce the same amount as lighter skin[^darling]. Dietary factors can also exacerbate this issue; vitamin D-rich foods such as fatty fish, fortified dairy, and eggs are consumed in limited quantities in many South Asian diets.

Physiological and genetic factors may also contribute to the persistence of deficiency. Genetic variation in key vitamin D-related genes has been shown to influence serum vitamin D levels and metabolism. For instance, Al-Daghri et al. identified significant ethnic differences in the frequency of vitamin D-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the GC and CYP2R1 genes, both of which play central roles in vitamin D transport and hydroxylation. In their study, South Asians exhibited higher minor allele frequencies in several GC gene variants, such as rs2282679 and rs7041, which were strongly associated with lower serum 25(OH)D concentrations. The CYP2R1 rs10741657 variant was also linked to reduced enzyme activity and decreased vitamin D synthesis among South Asians, suggesting a possible genetic predisposition to deficiency. These findings indicate that inherited differences in vitamin D metabolism may amplify the effects of environmental and cultural risk factors, creating a multi-faceted problem across the region[^aldaghri].

The health implications of chronic vitamin D deficiency are significant. Vitamin D influences immune function, calcium metabolism and bone health, and may play a role in the prevent of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and certain cancers[^darling]. The BCM Public Health meta-analysis also noted that widespread deficiency may be silently contributing to the growing prevalence of both infectious and chronic diseases in South Asia[^siddiqee]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that low vitamin D levels correlate with elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) concentrations and reduced bone mineral density, increasing the risk of osteomalacia, rickets, and osteoporosis[^shah]. These conditions are particularly concerning for women, children, and older adults, who already face increased vulnerability to nutritional and musculoskeletal disorders[^darling].

Addressing vitamin D deficiency in South Asia will require a coordinated, multi-level public-health response. Awareness campaigns that promote safe sun exposure and nutritional education are essential, especially among women and children. Policies supporting reinforcement via food, such as adding vitamin D to milk, flour, or cooking oil, could provide population-wide benefits at relatively low costs[^darling]. Routine screening for high-risk groups and targeted supplementation programs would further help reduce deficiency-related health disparities.

In conclusion, vitamin D deficiency represents a silent but ongoing public-health crisis in South Asia. The paradox of widespread deficiency in sun-abundant environments highlights how modern lifestyle, cultural practices, and biological factors are all contributing to this. Addressing this issue will require widespread public-health policy implementation and community engagement. By addressing this hidden epidemic, South Asian nations can make a meaningful step toward improving population health, preventing disease, and addressing one of the region’s most persistent nutritional gaps.
